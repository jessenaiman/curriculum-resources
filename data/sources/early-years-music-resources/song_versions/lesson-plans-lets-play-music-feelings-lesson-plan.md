# Lesson Plans

These lesson plans are intended to demonstrate how to put
together a structured 30 minute lesson using the songs and
activities from the Let’s Play Music website. Each week we will
take a different theme and aim to develop a broad base of
musical skills in singing, listening, percussion, pitch, rhythm and
understanding.
Ideally the songs should be sung together during the class with
some form of accompaniment, piano or guitar would be great
but kids are still perfectly happy just singing along together with
the percussion instruments instead.
If you work through the material for each lesson plan as listed, it
should last around 30 mins, but it is always important to be
ﬂexible. Sometimes one activity might be particularly successful
and you’ll want to do it for longer, or sometimes things don’t go
quite as planned, so it’s always important to have a few old
favourites ‘up your sleeve’. Circle games and Circle time
songs are always popular, and you can just slot them in if you
have a few spare minutes to ﬁll. Choose a quieter activity if they
need to settle or something lively if they need to work off the
wiggles!
The Hello Song which is at the beginning of every lesson can
also be used as a Goodbye Song, this works particularly well in a
music class where the children just come together for a weekly
session. It’s a great way to get started and bring the class to a
close and helps little ones to understand the routine. If the music
lesson is just a part of the normal school day, you may prefer to
leave it out.
In the interest of clarity and ease of use, I have tried to avoid
making these plans too wordy, and so only give in-depth
directions for the more unusual or original activities. However, all
the material can be found for reference on the Let’s Play Music
website. Search for the titles in bold using the search box on the
right hand side of the homepage, to check for more details.
Feelings
Focus - A lesson that encourages children to recognise and talk
about different feelings and emotions through a variety of
musical fun activities.
• Learning solfege with chime bars or resonator bells
• Playing a variety of percussion instruments, recognising the
steady beat and the rhythm.
• Listening and concentration skills
• Singing together with actions, new vocabulary, speech
development and solo responses.
• Social skills; working in a group, following instructions and
building conﬁdence.
• Imaginative movement to music, learning to connect music
with emotion and feelings and developing creativity .
Props needed:
• Resonator bells or a xylophone
• A variety of percussion instruments
• The printable sheet music and resources below.
• A cardboard staircase prop, to use with the little coloured
emotion characters.
• A selection of coloured scarves or ribbons and streamers to
use in musical movement.
• For the Sound game: Some small opaque containers (I use
old ﬁlm canisters, but plastic eggs/kinder eggs would work
well too)
• A collection of items that make a distinctive sound eg.:
pennies, shells, sugar, rice, bells etc. and a larger box to hide
them sound pots in
This lesson is suitable for children from 3 - 7 years and should
take around 35 mins, but many of the activities can be used again
separately whenever you need a short engaging, musical activity
at any other time. These activities are great for exploring and
recognising emotions when children ﬁnd it difﬁcult to express
their feelings.

2. The Do - Re - Mi Staircase with Emotion characters -
This game works well with the emotion stick puppets
included below. It teaches children the degrees of the solfege
scale  their correct order, the
solfege colours and how they sound.
Using a staircase to illustrate each step or note is an engaging
and visual way of teaching the spacial relationship between
notes and intervals in a way that children ﬁnd easy to
understand. It’s excellent preparation for learning to play an
instrument later on. In fact I’ve have found this game to be so
popular and useful as a teaching tool that it is really worth
including as a regular part of preschool music lessons.
See Introducing Chime Bars for more information about
teaching chime bars)
Y ou can make your own stairs by using our sheet below as a
template and mounting it on to strong card.  My own Do Re
Mi steps are cut out of wood, which you could do if you are
handy with a saw, or know someone who is. On the reverse
side of the staircase you need to make little pockets out of
duck tape behind each step so that you can slot the
characters into their correct position. Alternatively there is a
Bell Step Ladder available on Amazon (http://amzn.to/
23ksFjU) which would be more durable. Y ou could attach
our little emotion characters to wooden bricks which would
work very well and probably stand up to more use.
This game is a perfect way to introduce the emotion theme,
helping the kids to recognise and identify different feelings.
Once the children know which coloured emotion character
goes with which chime bar there are loads of different games
you can play:-
First make sure the children are familiar with the Do Re Mi
scale. Depending on the age of the children, take time to
introduce each character making sure the children are able to
recognise and name each colour and feeling and match it to
the correct note. This can be spread over several lessons.
Keep the chime bars hidden in a box or bag and ask the
children to help put the chime bars in the correct order.
Whilst you do this, place the little emotion characters onto
their corresponding stair on the staircase as the kids identify
each degree of the scale. (attach them to lolly sticks to make
them easier to slot in.)
Let the children take turns to guess which chime bar comes
next, and give them clues to prompt them
This is perfect if you have a group of children with mixed
abilities, as you only prompt when needed, depending on
their age and ability.
Start with low Do and work your way up the steps
until every note is in place.
Another very popular game is to hide the chime bars or little
emotion characters around the room and let the children
hunt for them, identifying them and putting them in the
correct order of the scale as they go.
3. If You’re happy And You Know It
Using the sheet music below, play and sing together with
piano  and percussion instruments.
1st time identify and accentuate the steady beat.
2nd time, identify and accentuate the rhythm (the same as
the words)
3rd time, choose just the wooden instruments to play.
4th time, choose just the bells and triangles.
Last time, play and sing loudly all together.
Put the instruments away, and sing the song with the actions.
Each verse add a new action (stamping feet and nodding
heads etc.), and at the end see if everyone can remember to
list them all one after the other.

5. Magic Feet Follow The Beat! By now the children will be
ready for some movement.Use the sheet below to play this
game, it’s a brilliant way to have some fun with some lively
movement, while learning and practising the concept of note
values.
Use a tambourine to tap the beat, and once they have
‘rubbed the magic on their feet’, they are really in the mood
to stamp around with giant steps like Daddy whole note
or march like Big Brother 1/4 note
or run along with little fast steps like The Little 1/8 Note
Twins . When they’ve got the hang of each different
note and the sound and action it makes, you can experiment
with making them guess which one you are playing and try to
catch them out, or each child can have a go at tapping the
correct beat when you call out the note.
6. How Are You Feeling Today? Guessing song with emotion
stick characters.
How are you feeling today?
Oh how are you feeling today?
I’m happy and glad, happy and glad,
That’s how I’m feeling today.
*
How are you feeling today?
Oh how are you feeling today?
I’m grumpy and cross, grumpy and cross,
That’s how I’m feeling today.
*
(repeat the verse with ‘sleepy and tired’, ‘frightened and scared’
or any other feelings that you might want to add like ‘I’m feeling
shy, scared, calm, tired etc)
This song is a great one for circle time, as it really engages the
children and they love trying to guess the different emotions.
Give each child a character to hold, and as you go round the
circle, the teacher sings the ﬁrst two lines, asking “How are you
feeling today?”  and each child identiﬁes which emotion their
stick puppet has and sings accordingly. They can use the same
words as above, but it really doesn’t matter, the whole point is
that they should say what they think and feel, and describe the
mood of the character that they are holding,
so encouraging improvisation and self-expression.
Keep it simple with just a couple of the more obvious emotions
to start with and gradually introduce the more challenging ones
once they get the hang of it.
Y ou can also prompt them with clues like ‘This is how you feel if
you see a monster!’ Encourage them to join in with giving the
clues – there is plenty of scope for some imaginative
improvisation!
Y ou can develop this game further by making it into a treasure
hunt. Hide the characters around the room and ask the children
to go searching for each one in turn. As you sing each verse (you
might have to repeat it a few times while their hunting!) they will
see all of the other characters on their way, and they must try to
remember where each one is.
