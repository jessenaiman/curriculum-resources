## Part 1: Core Principles with Exact Quotes

### 1.1 Definition of Prosody

> "Whatever you are saying, all of the elements should support it. That's basically common sense: to make sure that whatever you're trying to accomplish, all the things you use are in service of that goal." — *Sound on Sound interview*

> "Aristotle's may have been the first statement of Prosody: appropriate relationship between elements, whatever they may be: melody and words, chords and message, rhyme scheme and emotion, and many others. This has become the guiding principle in all my writing and teaching." — *Songwriting.net*

> "Prosody — the right relationship between form and content, first observed in great works of art by the poet Aristotle — is key." — *Sound on Sound*

From *Writing Better Lyrics* (p. 179): Prosody is defined as **"the appropriate relationship between elements, whatever they may be"** — every structural choice (number of lines, line length, rhyme scheme, rhyme type, rhythm, phrasing) should serve the song's central idea.

### 1.2 Structure as Emotional Architecture

> "The structure you create acts as a film score would adding additional emotion to the message, even controlling how the listener perceives it." — *Songwriting.net*

> "I talk about Prosody in terms of stability and instability, ideas that cover everything including your idea. If your concept is, 'I wish you were mine but you're not,' then the structure can make you feel that too." — *American Blues Scene interview*

> "Ask yourself, is the emotion in this section stable or unstable? Then build structure to match." — *Essential Guide to Lyric Form and Structure*

### 1.3 The Five Structural Elements

Pattison identifies **five structural tools** that create motion and emotion:

1. **Number of lines** (even = stable, odd = unstable)
2. **Length of lines** (matched = stable, unmatched = unstable)
3. **Rhythm of lines** (phrasing patterns)
4. **Rhyme scheme** (predictable patterns vs. surprising ones)
5. **Rhyme type** (perfect rhyme = most stable; consonance = least stable)

> "There are no rules, just tools." — *Sound on Sound*

### 1.4 Preserve the Natural Shape of Language

> "Mantra: Preserve the natural shape of the language." — *patpattison.com/language-and-songwriting*

> "A song is simply natural speech, exaggerated." — *patpattison.com*

> "Just as 'Is that tree real?' loses its meaning when *real* is wrenched out of its natural habitat in Ordinary Language, we lose meaning and emotion when we allow either musical rhythms or poetic rhythms to overpower the natural shape of words and phrases." — *patpattison.com*

This principle derives from Wittgenstein's ordinary language philosophy, which Pattison studied at Indiana University. His philosophical background shapes his approach: meaning lives in context, not abstraction.

### 1.5 Verbs as Power Amplifiers

> "Verbs are the power amplifiers of language. They drive it; set it in motion." — *patpattison.com*

> "There are many verbs — particularly the forms of 'to be': is, was, will be, and so on — that are very low-wattage and don't do anything. I'm simply recommending that the first thing you do is you learn to identify verbs and see if there's something a little stronger, so you can bump up the wattage. You don't want to pack a song so full that it's relentless." — *Sound on Sound*

### 1.6 Action Verbs and Sense-Bound Language

> "The more specific you get, the more sense-bound you get, the more effective it is." — *Sound on Sound*

> "If Nina Simone sings 'You'd be so nice by the fire,' I have a picture in my head that's drawn from my own experience. As a result, those words are actually about me, because they've stimulated my sense memories. That makes me feel something." — *Sound on Sound*

### 1.7 Rewriting as the Gold Standard

> "The reason you write something down is to have something to rewrite. Writing is always the key for professional songwriters, but rewriting is the gold standard." — *Sound on Sound*

> "90% of everything you write is not your best 10%, which I find incredibly comforting... Do not be afraid to write crap because crap is the best fertilizer." — *Berklee Online interview*

## Part 2: Specific Song Examples Used to Teach Prosody

### Example 1: John Mayer — "Your Body Is A Wonderland" (ODD NUMBER OF LINES = UNSTABLE)

**The Chorus:**
```
Your body is a wonderland
Your body is a wonder (I'll use my hands)
Your body is a wonderland…
```

Pattison's analysis: The chorus has only **3 lines** — an odd number. In his framework:
- Even numbers of lines → stable, complete, resolved
- Odd numbers → unstable, incomplete, wanting more

> "He drops you off the end of the building with the three-line chorus, making you want to go further. After the second verse, when they are getting it on, the second chorus, again uses three lines. It makes you feel unstable as you move forward." — *American Blues Scene*

**Prosody lesson**: The unstable 3-line chorus mirrors the speaker's desire — always reaching for something, never quite complete. The instability *is* the feeling of wanting.

---

### Example 2: Paul Simon — "Still Crazy After All These Years" (SHORTENED LINE = INSTABILITY)

Pattison highlights Paul Simon's **bridge** where the fourth line is conspicuously shorter than the others:

> "You could use an even number of lines and, say, shorten your fourth line, like Paul Simon does in his bridge to 'Still Crazy After All These Years'." — *Sound on Sound*

**Prosody lesson**: A shortened final line creates a sense of trailing off, of uncertainty — perfect for expressing the wistful doubt in "still crazy after all these years." The music stops but the thought hasn't finished.

---

### Example 3: Paul Simon — "Train in the Distance" (LENGTHENED LINE = INSTABILITY)

> "You could also lengthen your last line, which Paul Simon does in his bridge to 'Train in The Distance'." — *Sound on Sound*

**Prosody lesson**: Where the shortened line in "Still Crazy" feels like trailing off, the *lengthened* line in "Train in the Distance" feels like reaching further — stretching toward something out of reach. Both techniques achieve instability through opposite structural means.

---

### Example 4: Cole Porter — "You'd Be So Nice to Come Home To" (SENSE-BOUND LANGUAGE)

> "The title is an interesting look at how to say 'I love you'. This I find true in most of my teaching work over the years: the more specific you get, the more sense-bound you get, the more effective it is. The song's second line is 'You'd be so nice by the fire.'" — *Sound on Sound*

**Prosody lesson**: Instead of abstractly saying "I love you" or "I miss you," Porter paints a sensory picture — "by the fire" — that invites the listener to fill in the scene from their own experience. The listener becomes a *participant*, not an observer.

---

### Example 5: Mike Reid & Allen Shamblin — "I Can't Make You Love Me" (SENSORY DETAIL)

> "It starts: 'Turn down the lights, turn down the bed, turn down these voices inside my head.' Since it is so specific, it pulls the listener into the song." — *Sound on Sound*

**Scansion** (stressed syllables marked with capitalization and emphasis):
```
TURN down the LIGHTS, turn DOWN the BED,
turn DOWN these VOIces INside MY head.
```

**Prosody lesson**: Three concrete physical actions ("turn down") create rhythm through repetition, then pivot inward to psychological space ("voices inside my head"). The physical-to-emotional trajectory mirrors the song's emotional arc. The listener "sees the bed, knows what colour the bed cover is" — drawn from their own experience.

---

### Example 6: The Righteous Brothers — "You've Lost That Loving Feeling" (SENSORY OPENING)

> "'You never close your eyes any more when you kiss my lips': That's very compelling, because it stimulates me to unearth memories, so that I am a participant rather than an observer of the song." — *Sound on Sound*

**Scansion:**
```
you NEVer CLOSE your EYES aNYmore WHEN you KISS my LIPS
```

**Prosody lesson**: The opening line is a hyper-specific sensory observation — a detail that only someone intimately close would notice. This specificity creates instant emotional investment.

---

### Example 7: Irving Berlin — "What'll I Do" (COMPACT HEARTBREAK IN 32 BARS)

> "I like Irving Berlin's 'What'll I Do' a lot. It's a compact, skinny little song that just breaks your heart — in 32 bars." — *Sound on Sound*

**Why it works prosodically**: The song's brevity IS its emotional argument. There's no room for the speaker to elaborate, explain, or recover. The question "What'll I do?" is asked and left hanging — unstable, unresolved. The compact form mirrors the speaker's helplessness.

---

### Example 8: Robert Frost — "Putting In The Seed" (VERB POWER)

> "Robert Frost's poem 'Putting In The Seed', where a seedling is 'shouldering its way and shedding the earth crumbs'." — *Sound on Sound*

**Prosody lesson**: "Shouldering" and "shedding" are active, physical verbs that make the seedling seem alive with agency and effort. Compare with passive alternatives like "pushing through" or "coming out of" — the energy drops dramatically.

---

### Example 9: Keats — "Ode on a Grecian Urn" (RHYTHMIC SYNCOPATION SUPPORTING MEANING)

From Pattison's analysis on his website:

```
Thou FOSter CHILD of SIlence and SLOW TIME
da DUM da DUM da DUM da da DUM DUM
```

> "The diminuendo at 'da da' supports the concept of 'silence' — is an example of it. But even more remarkable, the double stress at 'slow time' slows the line down, supporting the concept of SLOW TIME!" — *patpattison.com*

**Contrast with bad prosody** — wrenching words to fit a rigid pattern:
```
Thou FOSter CHILD of Silence AND slow TIME
da DUM da DUM da DUM da DUM da DUM
```

> "If this were the way to create variation — by keeping the same meter and wrenching the words into an unnatural shape, we would lose the wonderful effects so hard won by diligent work and paying close attention. It would be the substitution of laziness and indifference for craft and talent." — *patpattison.com*

**Prosody lesson**: The syncopation (breaking the expected da-DUM pattern) isn't random — it's placed precisely where it amplifies meaning. The double stress at "slow time" literally slows the reader down.

---

### Example 10: "Eenie Meenie Miney Moe" vs. "Mary Had a Little Lamb" (MATCHED vs. UNMATCHED LINE LENGTH)

From Pattison's teaching (via American Blues Scene):

```
Eenie, meenie, minee, moe    ← equal length
Catch a tiger by the toe      ← equal length
```
→ **Feels very stable** — matched line lengths, predictable

```
Mary had a little lamb        ← shorter
Fleece as white as snow        ← longer
```
→ **Feels less stable** — unequal line lengths create forward motion

**Prosody lesson**: Equal-length lines feel contained and resolved. Unequal lines create tension and forward momentum.

---

### Example 11: "Can't Be Really Gone" (UNSTABLE STRUCTURE HIDING DENIAL)

From *Writing Better Lyrics* summary:

> "In 'Can't Be Really Gone,' a man insists his wife will return, but the verse uses an **odd number of lines, unequal lengths, no rhyme, and unresolved consonance**. The unstable structure quietly tells us he is in denial, even as his words claim otherwise."

**Prosody lesson**: The structure *contradicts* the surface meaning. The speaker says "she'll come back" (stable, certain), but the form says "I'm falling apart" (unstable, uncertain). The listener trusts the structure over the words.

---

### Example 12: John Mayer — "Stop This Train" (VERSE STRUCTURE AND EMOTIONAL ARC)

Pattison references this as a first-person narrative example of compelling songwriting.

> "If I'm going to tell the world about myself, it should be interesting to the world. The world should be able to take some lesson from my story. Otherwise, I'm just indulging myself." — *Sound on Sound*

**Prosody lesson**: The verse structure in "Stop This Train" matches the emotional content — the repetitive quality of the music mirrors the relentless forward motion of time that the speaker is desperately trying to stop.

---

### Example 13: Kelly Clarkson — "Unconditionally" (WORD-MUSIC PROSODY)

From Pattison's analysis of matching word stresses to musical beats:

```
unconDItional, unconDItionally
I will LOVE you unconDItionally
```

**Incorrect prosody** (stresses mismatched):
```
UncondiTIONal, uncondiTIONalLY
I will love YOU unCONdiTIONalLY
```

**Prosody lesson**: "Unconditional" has its primary stress on the third syllable (-di-). Setting it with stress on the wrong syllable ("TION") sounds forced and unnatural. Always match stressed syllables with musical downbeats/strong beats.

## Part 3: Common Prosody Mistakes Pattison Identifies

### Mistake 1: The "You'll Get Over It" Problem — Mismatched Structure and Emotion

> "I've seen it many times that somebody's singing a song on stage about a broken heart and the verses are four lines long, with equal-length lines that rhyme AABB. My response is: 'You'll get over it!' The heart doesn't sound broken, it sounds like you're just telling me facts!" — *Sound on Sound*

**The fix**: Use an **odd number of lines**, **unequal line lengths**, **asymmetrical rhyme schemes** (ABBA rather than AABB), or **unstable rhyme types** (assonance/consonance rather than perfect rhyme).

### Mistake 2: Wrenching Words to Fit a Musical Pattern

> "The variation is NOT achieved by keeping the same rhythmic pattern and mis-stressing syllables." — *patpattison.com*

> "If this were the way to create variation — by keeping the same meter and wrenching the words into an unnatural shape, we would lose the wonderful effects so hard won... It would be the substitution of laziness and indifference for craft and talent." — *patpattison.com*

**The fix**: Use the *natural* stresses of words to create rhythmic variation. Don't force syllables into unnatural positions.

### Mistake 3: Misaligned Word Stresses and Musical Stresses

> "Setting 'come back' like 'bridegroom' would be poor prosody — the musical stresses and the syllables' stresses would be opposite." — *patpattison.com*

```
BRIDE-groom  (STRONG-weak) — matches beat 1, beat 2
come BACK    (weak-STRONG) — opposite; setting this as STRONG-weak = bad prosody
```

**The fix**: Map word stresses to musical stresses. Stressed syllables go on downbeats/strong beats; unstressed syllables go on upbeats/weak beats.

### Mistake 4: Using Clichés Instead of Sense-Bound Language

> "The most common mistake is to use clichés: Cliché ideas, cliché rhymes, cliché phrases. Writing that comes not from an authentic part of the writer, but from prefabricated material that's been reassembled again and again. Your language can be simple and fresh without being cliché." — *Sound on Sound*

**The fix**: Replace abstract language with sensory specifics. Instead of "I love you," write "You'd be so nice by the fire." Instead of "I'm sad," write "Turn down the lights, turn down the bed."

### Mistake 5: Ignoring the "Film Score" Function of Structure

> "Somebody's singing a song on stage about a broken heart and the verses are four lines long, with equal-length lines that rhyme AABB." — *Sound on Sound*

**The fix**: Make your structural choices *deliberately*. Every structural element (number of lines, line length, rhyme scheme, phrasing) should be chosen to reinforce the emotion you're expressing.

### Mistake 6: Stressed Syllables in Unstressed Musical Positions

From Pattison's analysis of bad word-music alignment:

> "Look at all the places where stressed syllables appear in unstressed musical positions. These 'greedy' spots sound hurried and unnatural, calling attention away from the emotion of the line." — *patpattison.com*

**The fix**: When setting words to music, match the natural weight of syllables to the natural weight of beats.

### Mistake 7: Not Leaving Room for the Listener

> "The optimum way to do that is to put your most important lines in places that have a little space after them. The last line of your verse may be your biggest line in that verse, and then you're going through a two-bar turnaround for people to absorb it. Make sure that your most important ideas aren't competing with less important ideas for space." — *Sound on Sound*

**The fix**: Place your most important lines where there's breathing room after them — before choruses, before turnarounds, at section endings.

## Part 4: Exercises for Developing Prosody Skills

### Exercise 1: Object Writing (Daily Sensory Exercise)

**The Foundation Exercise** — Pattison's most famous contribution to songwriting pedagogy.

**How it works:**
1. Choose a random object, emotion, or event
2. Write for **10 minutes** without stopping
3. Focus on all **seven senses**: sight, smell, hearing, touch, taste, organic (internal sensation), kinesthetic (body movement)
4. Do this **every morning** — fresh mind, fresh senses

> "We wake up with fresh minds in the morning, cleansed from the sensory overload of the day before... your job as a writer is 'to master the art of diving'" — *Writing Better Lyrics*

**Purpose**: Trains you to think in sensory terms rather than abstract concepts. Over time, this becomes automatic, and your lyrics will naturally be more sense-bound and vivid.

---

### Exercise 2: Adjective-Noun Collisions (Group Exercise)

**Setup:** 4+ people. Group 1 writes 5 adjectives; Group 2 writes 5 nouns.

**Example lists:**
- Adjectives: smoky, refried, decaffeinated, hollow, understated
- Nouns: conversation, railroad, rainbow, rainforest, eyebrows

**Task:** Combine them and write a sentence or short paragraph:
- "Even the rainbow this afternoon has been decaffeinated"
- "smoky conversation"
- "hollow rainforest"

**Then jumble:** Combine across lists randomly (smoky eyebrows, understated railroad).

**Purpose:** Discovers accidental metaphors and trains the eye to see unexpected connections.

---

### Exercise 3: Verb-Noun Collisions (Group Exercise)

**Setup:** Same groups. Group 1 lists 5 verbs; Group 2 lists 5 nouns.

**Example:**
- Verbs: preaches, vomits, cancels, celebrates, palpitates
- Nouns: squirrel, wood stove, surfboard, reef, aroma

**Task:** "The red squirrel scrambled onto the branch, rose to his haunches and began **preaching** to us."

**Key insight:** "You get better results from Exercise 2 (nouns and verbs) than from Exercise 1 (adjectives and nouns). Verbs are the power amplifiers of language." — *patpattison.com*

---

### Exercise 4: Metaphor Finding (Two-Question Method)

For any idea, ask:
1. **What characteristics does this idea have?**
2. **What else has those characteristics?**

**Example for "tide":**
- Characteristics: rhythm, power, moon-driven, cycles, overwhelming, receding
- What else has those characteristics? → Ali, avalanche, army, electricity

**Resulting metaphors:**
- "Muhammad Ali avalanched over his opponents"
- "An avalanche is an army of snow"
- "A socket holds back tides of electricity"

---

### Exercise 5: Scansion Practice (Mapping Word Stresses to Music)

**How it works:**
1. Take a melody (or create one in 4/4 time)
2. Map which beats are strong (1, 3) and weak (2, 4)
3. Write lyrics that match: stressed syllables on strong beats, unstressed on weak
4. Check for "greedy" spots — places where stressed syllables land on weak beats

**Example from Pattison:**
```
BRIDE-groom → perfect match (STRONG-weak on beats 1-2)
come BACK → poor match if set as STRONG-weak; should be set as weak-STRONG
```

**Practice with Keats:**
```
Thou FOSter CHILD of SIlence and SLOW TIME
da DUM da DUM da DUM da da DUM DUM
```

---

### Exercise 6: Structure Mapping (Stability/Instability Analysis)

Take an existing song and analyze:
1. **Number of lines per section** — even or odd?
2. **Line lengths** — matched or varied?
3. **Rhyme scheme** — stable (AABB, ABAB) or unstable (ABBA)?
4. **Rhyme type** — perfect, family, assonance, consonance?
5. **Does the structure support the emotion?**

**Template questions:**
- Is this emotion stable or unstable?
- What does the number of lines tell me?
- What do the line lengths tell me?
- What does the rhyme scheme tell me?

---

### Exercise 7: Rewrite a Song's Structure

1. Take a verse with 4 equal lines and AABB rhyme (very stable)
2. Rewrite it with:
   - An odd number of lines (3 or 5)
   - Unequal line lengths
   - An ABBA or irregular rhyme scheme
3. Notice how the *feeling* changes even though the words stay mostly the same

---

### Exercise 8: Rhyme Type Ladder

Write the same couplet using all six of Pattison's rhyme types:

| Type | Example | Stability |
|------|---------|-----------|
| Perfect | cat / hat | Most stable |
| Family | rub / thud | Less stable |
| Additive | free / speed | Less stable |
| Subtractive | class / fast | Less stable |
| Assonance | hope / smoke | Least stable |
| Consonance | luck / lick | Least stable |

---

### Exercise 9: Point of View Testing

Take a lyric written in one point of view and rewrite it in all three:
1. **Third person** (he/she/they) — narrator outside the story
2. **First person** (I/me) — narrator inside the story
3. **Second person** (you) — direct address

Notice how each changes the emotional dynamic and the relationship between singer and listener.

## Part 5: The Stability/Instability Framework (Quick Reference)

### What Creates STABLE Feeling:
- Even number of lines (2, 4, 6, 8)
- Equal line lengths
- Predictable rhyme schemes (AABB, ABAB)
- Perfect rhyme
- Downbeat phrasing (phrases start on beat 1)
- Matched musical and verbal stresses

### What Creates UNSTABLE Feeling:
- Odd number of lines (3, 5, 7)
- Unequal line lengths (especially shortened or lengthened final line)
- Asymmetrical rhyme schemes (ABBA — "open" feeling)
- Remote rhyme types (assonance, consonance)
- Off-beat phrasing (phrases start after the downbeat)
- Mismatched or syncopated stresses

### When to Use Each:
| Emotion | Structure |
|---------|-----------|
| Contentment, resolution, love | Stable structure |
| Longing, uncertainty, loss | Unstable structure |
| Declaring, asserting | Front-heavy phrasing |
| Questioning, doubting | Back-heavy phrasing |

> "An even number of lines, a complete, tends to feel, my word, stable whereas an odd number of lines will feel odd, will feel incomplete, will feel unresolved, will feel unstable. So that the number of lines by themselves, I discovered, create a feeling." — *Songwriter Trysts podcast*
